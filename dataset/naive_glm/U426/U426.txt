This model describes a structural steel truss system, the kind of framework used in bridges, roof supports, or industrial gantries. Its primary function is to span a distance of 6 meters and safely support a heavy load of up to 100,000 Newtons (approximately 10 metric tons).

The physical structure is an assembly of 13 individual steel members connected at 8 specific joints, or nodes, to form a rigid, triangulated framework. This triangular design is crucial for efficiently distributing forces. The members are categorized as bottom chords, top chords, and diagonals, each with a specific length and cross-sectional area. The material specified is steel, which has defined properties for strength and stiffness. The entire truss assembly is designed to be anchored at two support nodes, which transfer the applied loads to a foundation.

Functionally, when a load is placed on the truss, the forces are channeled through the interconnected members. This puts each member into a state of either tension (being pulled apart) or compression (being pushed together). The system is designed to handle these internal forces and remain stable.

The system operates through several distinct states. It starts as 'unloaded'. When a load is applied, the system enters a 'loading' state where a structural analysis is performed. This analysis calculates the stress within each member and the overall bending (deflection) of the entire structure. If the stresses and deflection are within predefined safety limits, the truss is considered 'operational' and is safely carrying the load. However, if the analysis predicts that any member will be overstressed or the structure will bend too much, it enters an 'overstressed' state, indicating a high risk of structural failure. Once the load is removed from an operational truss, it returns to the 'unloaded' state.

This model describes an aircraft Attitude Indicator, commonly known as an artificial horizon. Its primary function is to provide pilots with a clear, real-time visual representation of their aircraft's orientation (pitch and roll) relative to the Earth's horizon. This is crucial for maintaining control and spatial awareness, especially when flying in clouds or at night where the natural horizon is not visible.

The system is composed of several integrated components that work together:

*   **Sensors:** The core of the system relies on two types of sensors. A **Rate Gyroscope** measures how quickly the aircraft is rotating its nose up or down (pitch rate) and banking its wings (roll rate). A **Three-Axis Accelerometer** measures linear forces, which allows it to determine the constant direction of gravity, providing a stable reference for which way is "down."

*   **Attitude Computer:** This is the brain of the instrument. It receives continuous data from both the gyroscope and the accelerometer. It then "fuses" these two streams of information using a complementary filter. This process intelligently combines the fast, responsive data from the gyroscope with the stable, long-term reference from the accelerometer to calculate a single, highly accurate and reliable measurement of the aircraft's current pitch and roll angles.

*   **Horizon Display:** This is the visual interface for the pilot. It receives the calculated attitude data from the computer and presents it on a screen. The display typically shows a symbolic aircraft fixed in the center over a moving background that depicts the sky and ground, with a clear horizon line. Graduated markings (a pitch ladder and roll scale) allow the pilot to read the exact degrees of pitch and bank.

*   **Power Management Unit:** This component ensures the entire system operates reliably. It takes in power from the aircraft's main electrical system (e.g., 28V DC), performs a self-test upon startup, and then regulates and distributes the correct, stable voltage to each of the other components—the sensors, computer, and display. It also monitors the input voltage to handle low-power situations.

In operation, the gyroscope and accelerometer constantly sense the aircraft's movements. This raw sensor data flows to the attitude computer, which processes it to determine the precise orientation. This final attitude data is then sent to the horizon display, which updates dozens of times per second to provide the pilot with a smooth, continuous, and intuitive picture of the aircraft's flight attitude. The entire system is designed to operate in various states, including an "off" state, a self-test during startup, a normal operational mode, and even a "degraded" mode if a sensor issue is detected.

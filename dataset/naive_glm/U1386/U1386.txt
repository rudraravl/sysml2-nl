This model describes a biomimetic adhesion system, essentially a robotic gripper that mimics the remarkable sticking ability of a gecko's foot. It's designed for applications like climbing robots or for handling objects in manufacturing, allowing for strong, controllable attachment to surfaces without using any adhesives or suction.

The core of the system is a set of adhesive pads that generate grip through van der Waals forces, the same principle used by geckos. These pads are not just simple sticky surfaces; they have a complex, hierarchical structure. Each pad consists of several smaller, finger-like "toes." Each of these toes is covered in flexible, layered flaps called lamellae. The surface of these lamellae is coated with a dense array of millions of microscopic, hair-like fibers known as setae. This multi-layered, flexible design allows the pad to conform perfectly to a surface, maximizing contact even if the surface isn't perfectly flat.

The system's operation is a carefully controlled cycle. To attach, the gripper approaches a surface and then engages by applying a normal force (pressing onto it) and a slight shearing force. This action brings the vast number of microscopic setae into intimate contact with the surface, activating the powerful adhesion. Once adhered, it can securely support a significant load.

Detachment is just as sophisticated. Instead of pulling straight off, the system uses an angle controller to change the "peel angle" of the toes, lifting them away from the surface from an edge. This peeling motion sequentially breaks the millions of tiny contact points, allowing the gripper to release its grip with very little effort.

The entire process is managed through a series of states: it can be detached, approaching a surface, actively engaging its grip, securely adhered while monitoring the load, or actively releasing. For safety, the system includes an overload protection feature; if the load exceeds its maximum capacity, it can trigger an emergency release to prevent failure.

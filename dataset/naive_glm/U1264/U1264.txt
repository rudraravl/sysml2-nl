This describes an X10 home automation system, a classic technology designed to control electrical devices remotely by sending signals over a home's existing electrical wiring. This eliminates the need for new, dedicated control wires.

The system is composed of a central controller, a transceiver, a power line interface, and various receiver modules that are connected to individual lights and appliances.

The core of the system's operation is as follows:
1.  A user interacts with a **main controller** (like a remote or a keypad) to issue a command, such as "turn on the living room lamp."
2.  The controller generates a digital command packet. Each packet contains a unique address, consisting of a "House Code" (e.g., "A") and a "Unit Code" (e.g., "1"), along with a specific function like 'on', 'off', 'dim', or 'bright'.
3.  A **transceiver** takes this digital command and modulates it into a high-frequency (120 kHz) signal.
4.  This signal is injected by a **power line interface** onto the home's standard 120V/60Hz AC electrical wiring.
5.  The signal travels throughout the house's wiring to all connected **X10 modules**.

There are several types of specialized modules designed for different devices:
*   **Lamp Modules:** These are for controlling lights. They can turn lamps on and off and also adjust their brightness (dimming). They are typically rated for loads up to 300 watts.
*   **Appliance Modules:** Designed for devices without dimming capabilities, like fans or coffee makers. They function as simple on/off switches and can handle higher-power loads, up to 1800 watts.
*   **Wall Switches:** These replace standard in-wall light switches, allowing built-in lighting to be part of the automation system while still permitting local, manual control.

Each module is assigned a unique address. When a command is broadcast over the power lines, all modules "hear" it, but only the module whose House and Unit codes match the address in the command will execute the function. This allows for the precise control of up to 256 individual devices within a single home. The transceiver is also capable of receiving signals, enabling two-way communication for status reporting and more advanced control scenarios.

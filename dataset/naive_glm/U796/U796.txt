This model describes an optode, a high-precision optical sensor designed to measure the concentration of dissolved oxygen in liquids, typically water. The system operates on the principle of fluorescence quenching, where the presence of oxygen affects the light emitted by a special chemical dye.

The sensor is a self-contained unit composed of several interconnected components, all powered by an internal power supply. The measurement process begins when an LED light source generates a specific pulse of blue light. This light travels through a delivery optical fiber to the sensor tip, which houses a fluorescent indicator dye. This tip is the part of the system that is placed directly into the liquid sample.

When the blue light from the LED hits the dye, the dye becomes excited and emits its own light (fluoresces) in response. The crucial part of this process is that the dissolved oxygen in the sample interferes with, or "quenches," this fluorescence. The more oxygen present, the shorter and weaker the fluorescent signal becomes.

A second optical fiber, the collection fiber, captures this emitted light and guides it to a photodetector. The photodetector measures the precise characteristics of the fluorescence, specifically its intensity and decay time.

These raw measurements are then sent to an onboard signal processing unit, which acts as the brain of the sensor. This processor uses the fluorescence decay time, along with simultaneous readings from integrated temperature and pressure sensors, to calculate the exact concentration of dissolved oxygen. The system actively compensates for temperature and pressure to ensure the final reading is highly accurate.

Finally, a data interface formats this complete, calibrated oxygen reading—which includes the concentration, temperature, pressure, and a timestamp—and transmits it to an external monitoring system or data logger, typically using an RS-485 communication protocol. The entire cycle repeats to provide continuous, real-time measurements.

This model represents a natural hydrological watershed system, specifically a single landform named Donald Hill. The system is described in terms of its physical structure and its response to rainfall events, focusing on processes like water absorption, surface runoff, and soil erosion.

The hill itself is composed of three distinct segments: an upper, middle, and lower slope, each with its own elevation, steepness, and surface area. Overlaying this physical terrain are two key ecological components: a layer of vegetation and a profile of the soil. The vegetation, characterized by its density, acts as the first line of defense against rainfall by intercepting a portion of it with its canopy. The soil has properties like thickness and permeability, which determine how quickly it can absorb water and how much it can hold before becoming saturated.

When a rainfall event occurs, the system becomes active. Water that isn't caught by the vegetation reaches the ground and begins to soak into the soil. If the rain is too heavy or the ground becomes saturated, excess water begins to flow over the surface as runoff. This runoff travels downhill, moving from the upper segment to the middle, and then to the lower segment. As the water flows, it can dislodge and carry away soil, causing erosion. The amount of erosion depends on the volume of water, the hill's slope, and the protective effect of the vegetation cover.

Finally, all the surface runoff is collected into a natural drainage network of channels or gullies, which routes the water and accumulated sediment off the main hillslope. The entire system cycles through different states depending on the weather, from a dry "idle" state, to "absorbing" and "saturating" during a storm, potentially "eroding" during heavy flow, and finally "draining" and returning to idle once the rain has stopped.

This model represents a modern economic system designed to analyze and understand the phenomenon of a "jobless recovery." This occurs when a nation's economy, as measured by GDP, begins to grow again after a recession, but the unemployment rate remains high because job creation does not keep pace. The system shows that this growth is primarily driven by gains in productivity from technology and automation, rather than by hiring more people.

The system is composed of several key interacting sectors that represent a real-world economy:

*   The **Corporate Sector** represents the businesses that drive economic output. They make critical decisions on whether to meet rising demand by hiring more workers or by investing in new, more efficient technology.
*   The **Labor Market** consists of the workforce, including both employed and unemployed individuals. Its health is directly tied to the hiring decisions made by the Corporate Sector.
*   The **Technology Sector** develops and supplies the automation and productivity-enhancing tools that corporations can purchase as an alternative to labor.
*   A **Policy Authority**, such as a government or central bank, monitors the economy's health. When it detects problematic trends like a jobless recovery, it can intervene with policies like fiscal stimulus, infrastructure investment, or funding for workforce retraining programs.
*   An **Economic Monitor** acts as an impartial observer, collecting and distributing key data like GDP growth, unemployment rates, and productivity levels to all other sectors, enabling them to make informed decisions.

The system operates by simulating the economic cycle. Following a recession, as demand begins to recover, the Corporate Sector must decide how to increase its output. If the cost of new technology, adjusted for its productivity benefits, is lower than the cost of hiring new workers, businesses will choose to automate. This action boosts economic output and corporate profits, but it fails to create new jobs, leading the system into a "jobless recovery" state. The Policy Authority can then react to the high unemployment by introducing measures aimed at stimulating job growth or re-skilling the workforce to better fit the needs of a more automated economy.

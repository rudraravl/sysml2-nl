This model describes an ion-selective electrode (ISE) system, a scientific instrument designed to measure the concentration of a specific ion, such as sodium, potassium, or chloride, in a liquid sample.

The system is centered around an electrode assembly that is immersed in the solution being tested. The core of this assembly is a selective membrane, a specialized material that generates an electrical potential (voltage) that changes in direct proportion to the concentration of the target ion. To get an accurate reading, this potential is measured against a stable, unchanging voltage provided by a separate reference electrode.

A high-impedance potential meter, essentially a sensitive voltmeter, measures the tiny voltage difference between the selective membrane and the reference electrode. This raw electrical signal is then cleaned up and amplified by a signal processor to remove noise. Finally, a calculator unit takes this processed voltage, applies a scientific formula (the Nernst equation) along with calibration data, and converts it into a precise ion concentration reading.

The system operates through several states. It starts in an initialization mode for self-checks. Before use, it must be put into a calibration state where it is tested against solutions of known concentration to ensure accuracy. Once calibrated, it is in a ready state. During analysis, it enters a measuring state, which may include a stabilizing period to wait for the electrical reading to become steady before a final result is calculated. The system can also enter a maintenance state if it detects that its readings have drifted or if it requires cleaning, after which it must be re-initialized and calibrated.

This model describes a Thermoluminescent Dosimeter (TLD) system, which is used to measure the cumulative dose of ionizing radiation received by a person or an area over a period of time. The system is primarily used for personnel safety monitoring in environments with radiation, such as nuclear power plants, hospitals, and research laboratories.

The system consists of two main physical components: a passive, wearable **Dosimeter Badge** and an active **TLD Reader** device.

The **Dosimeter Badge** is a small, portable device worn by personnel. At its core is a **TLD Crystal**, a special material that absorbs energy from radiation exposure. When irradiated, electrons within the crystal's lattice structure become trapped in an excited state, effectively storing a record of the total radiation dose received. This process is passive and requires no power; the badge simply accumulates the dose as long as it is exposed.

The **TLD Reader** is the analytical instrument used to determine the dose stored in the badge. To do this, the badge is inserted into the reader. The reader contains a precision **Heating Element** that warms the TLD crystal according to a specific temperature profile. As the crystal is heated, the trapped electrons are released, and in returning to their normal state, they emit a faint glow of light. The intensity of this emitted light is directly proportional to the amount of radiation the crystal had previously absorbed.

This light is captured by a highly sensitive **Photomultiplier Tube (PMT)** within the reader, which converts the light photons into a measurable electrical signal. A **Control Processor** then analyzes this electrical signal, integrates it over the heating cycle, and uses pre-loaded calibration data to calculate the exact radiation dose. The final result is a formal **Dose Record**, which includes the measured dose, the time of the reading, and the unique ID of the badge.

A key feature of the system is its reusability. After the reading is complete, the reader performs an "annealing" process by heating the crystal to a higher temperature. This erases any residual signal, effectively resetting the badge to zero and preparing it to be reissued for another monitoring period. The lifecycle of a badge involves being issued to personnel, accumulating a dose in the field, being collected for analysis, having its dose read, and finally being annealed for reuse.

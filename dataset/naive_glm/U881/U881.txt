This model describes a Single-Photon Emission Computed Tomography (SPECT) system, a sophisticated medical imaging device used for creating three-dimensional images of physiological processes within the human body. It provides a functional map of organs and tissues, unlike anatomical imaging like X-rays or CT scans.

The system's operation begins when a patient receives an injection of a radiopharmaceutical, a special radioactive drug designed to accumulate in a specific organ or tissue of interest. As this drug decays, it emits gamma photons, which travel out of the patient's body. The SPECT system's primary function is to detect these emitted photons from multiple angles and reconstruct an image showing the drug's distribution.

The central hardware consists of one or more specialized detectors, called Gamma Cameras, mounted on a rotating structure called a Gantry. This gantry slowly rotates around the patient, who lies still during the scan. Each Gamma Camera is a complex assembly designed to precisely detect gamma photons. First, photons pass through a Collimator, typically a lead plate with thousands of tiny, parallel holes, which filters out scattered photons and only allows those traveling in a specific direction to reach the detector.

After passing through the collimator, the photons strike a large Scintillation Crystal (commonly made of Sodium Iodide). This crystal converts the high-energy gamma photon into a faint flash of visible light. An array of Photomultiplier Tubes (PMTs) positioned behind the crystal detects this light, amplifies it significantly, and converts it into an electrical signal. A Position Logic Circuit then uses the signals from multiple PMTs to calculate the exact location where the photon hit the crystal.

As the gantry rotates, the Gamma Cameras capture a series of 2D images, known as projections, from many different angles. These raw signals are processed by a Data Acquisition System, which digitizes them and uses an Energy Discriminator to accept only photons within a specific energy window, further improving image quality by rejecting scatter.

Once the scan is complete, an Image Reconstruction System takes all the 2D projection data. Using powerful computer algorithms (such as Filtered Backprojection or iterative methods), it reconstructs a full 3D dataset. This system also applies crucial corrections for physical effects like photon attenuation (absorption by the body) and scatter, which significantly improves the final image's accuracy.

The entire process is managed from an Operator Console. From here, a technician selects the appropriate scanning protocol, initiates calibration and scanning procedures, and views the final reconstructed 3D images on a high-resolution display for diagnostic review by a physician. The system operates through distinct states, including idle, calibrating, active scanning (which involves both acquiring and reconstructing data), and ready for the next patient.

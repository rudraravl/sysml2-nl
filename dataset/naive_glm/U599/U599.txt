This model describes a highly specialized optical sensor, an individual "angle-sensitive pixel," designed to determine not only the intensity of light but also the precise direction from which it is coming. This technology could be a building block for advanced imaging systems, such as 3D or light-field cameras.

The system's core operation relies on a clever dual-channel approach. Incoming light first passes through a microlens which focuses it. Immediately after the lens, the light path is split and directed toward two different diffraction gratings. These gratings are specifically engineered with slightly different properties, causing them to bend the light in unique, predictable ways based on its angle of incidence.

Each of these two light paths terminates at a dedicated photodetector, which converts the light it receives into an electrical signal. Because the two gratings manipulate the light differently, the intensity of the signal produced by each photodetector will vary based on the original angle of the incoming light.

An onboard angle computation unit receives the electrical signals from both photodetectors. By comparing the difference in the two signal strengths and referencing an internal calibration matrix, this processor can accurately calculate the original angle of the light. The final output is a data packet containing the measured angle, a confidence level for the measurement, and a timestamp.

The pixel is designed to be power-efficient, operating in different states. It can remain in a low-power "idle" mode, transition to a "ready" state upon detecting light, and finally enter a full-power "measuring" state to perform its angle calculation. This allows it to function effectively as part of a larger sensor array.

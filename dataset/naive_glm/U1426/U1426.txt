This model represents the Hengsteysee Hydropower System, a real-world facility located on the Ruhr River in Germany. Its primary purpose is to generate renewable hydroelectric power while also performing crucial civil functions like regulating the river's flow and controlling potential flooding.

The core of the system is a large concrete dam that creates the Hengsteysee reservoir. This reservoir has a defined capacity and operational water levels that must be carefully managed. To generate power, water from the reservoir is channeled through two hydroelectric turbines (a main and an auxiliary unit). As water flows through the turbines, it spins them to generate electricity, which is then supplied to the main power grid. The turbines can be in various states, such as running, stopped, or in the process of starting or stopping.

For water level and flood management, the system uses several components in addition to the turbines. A primary, controllable sluice gate allows operators to release water directly from the reservoir without passing it through the turbines. An emergency sluice gate provides a redundant method for water discharge, and a large spillway acts as a passive safety feature, automatically releasing water if the reservoir level exceeds a critical height, preventing the dam from being overtopped.

The entire operation is orchestrated by a central control center. This center continuously monitors the system's health and environment using a suite of sensors, including water level sensors both upstream and downstream, and flow meters measuring the water entering and leaving the reservoir. Based on this data, the control center adjusts the turbine operation and the opening of the sluice gates.

The system operates in different modes depending on conditions. In "normal" mode, it prioritizes efficient power generation while maintaining a target water level. During periods of heavy rainfall, it enters a "flood control" mode to discharge excess water safely and prevent downstream flooding. Conversely, in a "low water" situation, it conserves water to ensure a minimum environmental flow and prepare for future power generation. This dynamic management ensures the system balances its multiple responsibilities, from power production and structural safety to environmental protection.

This model describes the **CancerVision Goggles**, a wearable surgical visualization system designed to help surgeons identify and remove cancerous tissue with high precision during an operation.

The system is a self-contained, battery-powered headset that the surgeon wears. Its primary function is to provide a real-time, augmented-reality view of the surgical field, where cancerous cells are highlighted directly in the surgeon's line of sight.

Here’s how it works:
1.  **Illumination:** The goggles are equipped with a near-infrared (NIR) light source. This light, which is invisible to the human eye, illuminates the tissue in the surgical area. It's designed to excite a special fluorescent dye that is administered to the patient beforehand and accumulates in cancer cells.
2.  **Detection:** A specialized fluorescence camera, also built into the goggles, is tuned to detect the faint glow (fluorescence) emitted by the cancer cells when they are hit by the NIR light. This camera captures a high-definition video feed of this fluorescence.
3.  **Processing & Analysis:** The video is sent to an onboard Image Processing Unit, which enhances the signal. This enhanced video is then analyzed in real-time by a powerful Tumor Detection Engine. Using an advanced machine learning model, this engine identifies the precise boundaries of the tumor, calculates resection margins, and assigns a confidence score to its findings.
4.  **Visualization:** The system then generates a digital overlay that highlights the identified cancerous regions. This overlay is combined with the normal video feed and projected onto the high-resolution Head-Mounted Display inside the goggles. This provides the surgeon with an augmented reality view, allowing them to see exactly which tissue to remove and which healthy tissue to spare.

The entire system is powered by an integrated battery pack designed for several hours of continuous operation. The goggles operate in different states, including a low-power standby mode, a calibration phase before use, and the active imaging mode for surgery. The system is engineered for very low latency, ensuring the visual overlay is perfectly synchronized with the surgeon's movements and the real-world view.

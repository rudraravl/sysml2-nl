This model represents the Chernobyl Unit 4 RBMK-1000 nuclear power reactor system, a facility designed to generate electricity through nuclear fission. The model details not only the physical components of the reactor but also simulates the specific operational sequence and design flaws that led to the catastrophic accident in 1986.

The core of the system is the **RBMK-1000 Reactor Core**, which contains 1661 fuel channels and uses a graphite moderator. A key, and dangerous, characteristic of this design is its "positive void coefficient," meaning that as the water coolant turns to steam (voids), the nuclear reaction actually speeds up rather than slowing down, making it inherently unstable at low power levels.

The system's power output is managed by a **Control Rod Assembly** consisting of 211 rods. A critical design flaw is represented here: the rods feature a boron carbide section to absorb neutrons and shut down the reaction, but they also have graphite displacer tips. During an emergency shutdown, as the rods are inserted, these graphite tips initially displace water, causing a brief but catastrophic surge in reactivity before the absorbing material can take effect.

Heat from the reactor is managed by the **Primary Coolant Circuit**, which pumps water through the core. This water boils, and the resulting steam-water mixture is sent to **Steam Separator Drums**. These drums separate the high-pressure steam from the water; the steam is piped to drive a **Turbine Generator Set** to produce electricity, while the water is recirculated back into the coolant circuit.

Overseeing the entire operation are two key components: the **Operator Control Room** and the **Emergency Protection System**. The control room is the human interface, allowing operators to monitor reactor status and issue commands. The Emergency Protection System is designed to automatically shut down the reactor (a "scram") if unsafe conditions like excessive power or low coolant flow are detected.

The model specifically captures the scenario of the fateful safety test. In this sequence, the reactor is deliberately put into its unstable low-power state. To conduct the test, operators in the control room override and disable critical automatic safety systems. As the test proceeds, a power surge begins due to the formation of steam voids. When operators attempt an emergency shutdown, the flawed control rod design causes a massive, instantaneous power spike, leading to a steam explosion that ruptures the reactor, followed by a second explosion and a devastating graphite fire.

This model represents a Sextant Navigation System, a classic, high-precision optical instrument used for celestial navigation, typically at sea. Its primary function is to determine a person's geographic position (latitude and longitude) by accurately measuring the angular height of a celestial body—such as the sun, moon, or a star—above the visible horizon.

The physical instrument consists of a sturdy frame, often made of brass, which holds the various optical and mechanical components. The core of its operation involves a system of mirrors. A telescope provides the user with a magnified view. An "index mirror" is mounted on a movable "index arm," which captures the image of the celestial body. This reflected image is then directed onto a "horizon glass," which is half-silvered and half-clear. This setup allows the navigator to see two images simultaneously: the reflected celestial body through the mirrored portion and the actual horizon through the clear portion.

To take a measurement, or "sight," the navigator adjusts the index arm, swinging the mirror until the image of the celestial body appears to be resting perfectly on the horizon line. Shading filters can be used to reduce the intensity of bright objects like the sun. Once the images are aligned, the arm is locked, and the precise angle is read from a primary "arc scale" and a "micrometer drum" for fine-tuning.

The process of finding one's location involves taking at least two such sights, carefully recording the angle and the exact time for each. These raw measurements are then mathematically adjusted for various known errors (like instrument imperfections, the observer's height above sea level, and atmospheric refraction). The corrected data from these sights is used to calculate lines of position, and the point where these lines intersect on a nautical chart reveals the navigator's geographic fix. The instrument has distinct operational states, moving from stowed, to ready, through the various steps of sighting, and finally to a locked state when a measurement is captured.

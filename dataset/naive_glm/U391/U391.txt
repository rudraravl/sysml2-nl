This model describes a sphygmograph, a mechanical medical instrument designed to create a graphical representation of the human arterial pulse. Its purpose is to physically detect, amplify, and record the subtle movements of an artery as blood pulses through it, producing a visual chart called a pulse waveform for analysis.

The system is composed of several interconnected mechanical components that work in sequence. It begins with a **Pulse Sensor**, which is placed directly on the skin over an artery (like the radial artery in the wrist). This sensor detects the small physical throbbing of the artery wall.

The tiny mechanical movement from the sensor is then transferred to a **Lever Amplifier**. This is a system of levers designed to magnify the small, almost imperceptible pulse movements into a much larger, more visible motion.

This amplified motion drives a **Stylus Assembly**, which is essentially a writing pen or needle. As the pulse pushes the lever system, the stylus moves up and down in unison.

Simultaneously, a **Rotating Drum**, covered with a long strip of paper, turns at a constant, steady speed. The moving stylus traces a continuous line onto this moving paper. The combination of the stylus's vertical movement (representing the pulse amplitude) and the paper's horizontal movement (representing time) creates the final pulse waveform trace. The system also includes a **Calibration Scale** to provide reference marks for time and amplitude on the recording, allowing a doctor to accurately measure the pulse rate and waveform characteristics from the chart.

The sphygmograph operates through distinct phases. It starts in an idle state. Upon receiving a command, it enters a setup phase where the operator positions the sensor on the patient and prepares the paper on the drum. Once the sensor makes proper contact, the device begins recording. During this active phase, the drum rotates and the stylus traces the live pulse onto the paper. After a set duration, the recording is complete, and the device stops, leaving a permanent graphical record of the patient's pulse for examination.

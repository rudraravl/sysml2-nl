This is an electron multiplier system, a highly sensitive detector designed to amplify a single incoming electron into a large, easily measurable electrical pulse. It is commonly used in scientific instruments like mass spectrometers, scanning electron microscopes, and particle detectors where signals are extremely weak.

The system's core is a cascade of ten amplifying plates called dynodes, all housed within a high-vacuum chamber. This vacuum is critical to ensure that electrons can travel freely without colliding with air molecules. The entire process is driven by a stable high-voltage power supply, which creates a strong electric field between the dynodes.

Operation begins when a single electron enters the detector and is guided to strike the first dynode. The impact causes the dynode's surface to release several new "secondary" electrons. This small group of electrons is then accelerated by the electric field towards the second dynode. When they strike it, each one again releases multiple secondary electrons, further multiplying the total number. This process repeats in an avalanche-like cascade down the entire chain of ten dynodes.

By the end of the cascade, the initial single electron has been multiplied into a pulse containing millions of electrons. This large electron pulse is collected by a final component called an anode, which converts the charge into a distinct electrical current and voltage signal. This final, amplified signal is strong enough to be processed by standard electronics, effectively allowing the detection of a single particle event.

This model describes a Daly Detector System, a highly sensitive scientific instrument designed for detecting and counting individual ions, commonly used in applications like mass spectrometry. Its primary function is to take a single, low-energy ion, which is difficult to measure directly, and convert it into a large, easily countable electrical pulse.

The entire detector assembly is housed within a vacuum chamber to ensure that the particles can travel without interference from air molecules. The detection process works in a multi-stage cascade:

1.  **Ion-to-Electron Conversion:** An incoming ion first strikes a metal plate known as a **conversion dynode**, which is held at a very high negative voltage (e.g., -20,000 volts). This impact knocks a shower of secondary electrons out of the metal surface.

2.  **Electron-to-Photon Conversion:** These electrons are then accelerated towards a **scintillator**, which is a screen coated with a phosphor material. When the electrons hit the scintillator, it emits a brief, faint flash of light (photons).

3.  **Photon Amplification:** The flash of light is detected by a **photomultiplier tube (PMT)**, an extremely sensitive light detector. The PMT amplifies this weak optical signal by a factor of a million or more, converting it into a significant electrical pulse.

4.  **Signal Processing:** This raw electrical pulse is sent to a **signal processor**. This electronic unit cleans up the signal, filters out background noise by ignoring pulses below a certain threshold, and officially counts it as a valid ion detection event. The final, conditioned pulse is then sent out as the system's output.

To operate correctly, the system relies on a dedicated **high-voltage supply** that provides the stable, high-potential electrical fields needed for both the conversion dynode and the photomultiplier tube.

The system operates in several distinct modes, including an **idle** state when not in use, an **active** state for detection, a **calibration** mode for setup and tuning, and a **fault** state if a problem is detected. This allows for controlled operation, maintenance, and reliable performance.

This is a model of a Wire Chamber, a sophisticated particle detector system used in physics experiments to track the trajectory of charged particles. Its primary function is to detect when a charged particle passes through it and to precisely measure its position and time of arrival.

The core of the system is the detector chamber itself, a sealed volume filled with a specific gas mixture, typically Argon and CO2. This gas chamber contains a precise arrangement of electrodes: two flat cathode planes held at ground potential and, sandwiched between them, a plane of 128 extremely thin, parallel anode wires. A dedicated gas flow controller ensures the chamber is constantly supplied with the correct gas mixture at a stable pressure.

A high-voltage module supplies a stable, high positive voltage (around 2800 volts) to the anode wires, creating a strong electric field within the gas. When a charged particle flies through the chamber, it collides with gas atoms and knocks electrons loose, creating a trail of electron-ion pairs. Pulled by the electric field, these free electrons drift towards the nearest high-voltage anode wires. As they get extremely close to a wire, the intense field causes an "avalanche," rapidly multiplying the number of electrons.

This avalanche of charge creates a tiny, measurable electrical current pulse on the specific wire it hit. This raw signal is the first indication of the particle's passage. Each of the 128 wires is connected to its own electronics channel to process this signal. First, a charge amplifier boosts the weak signal. Next, a discriminator cleans up the amplified signal, ignoring low-level noise and converting a valid detection into a clean digital pulse.

A high-precision time digitizer then measures the exact arrival time of this digital pulse for each wire that was hit, with a resolution of a few nanoseconds. Finally, a data acquisition (DAQ) system collects all the timing and wire-number information from the digitizer. By analyzing which wires were triggered and in what sequence, the DAQ system can reconstruct the original particle's path through the detector, creating a final data record of the event.

The system is designed for high performance, capable of achieving a spatial resolution of 400 micrometers, a time resolution of 3 nanoseconds, and handling particle rates of up to 1.5 million per second with over 98% detection efficiency. The detector operates in several states, including standby, armed for detection, and active data readout.

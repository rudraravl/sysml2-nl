This model describes a ChemFET (Chemical Field-Effect Transistor) sensing system, a type of electronic sensor designed to detect and measure the concentration of specific chemical substances in a solution. Its primary function is to convert the presence of a target chemical into a proportional and measurable electrical signal.

The system is composed of four main components that work in sequence. First, an **Ion-Selective Membrane** acts as the chemical interface. This specialized surface is designed to be highly selective, meaning it preferentially binds with a specific target chemical (the analyte) from the solution. When the chemical binds to it, the membrane's surface electrical potential changes.

This change in potential is detected by the core of the sensor, a **Field-Effect Transistor (FET) Transducer**. This electronic component uses the voltage change from the membrane as a control signal. A stable voltage, provided by a **Reference Electrode**, serves as a baseline, allowing the system to accurately measure the change caused by the chemical interaction. The FET acts as an amplifier; the small voltage change at the membrane modulates a much larger electrical current flowing through the transistor.

Finally, a **Readout Circuit** processes the current from the FET. It converts this current into a more easily measured voltage, amplifies it, and filters out electronic noise to produce a clean, stable output signal.

In operation, the sensor goes through distinct states, starting in an idle mode, proceeding to a calibration phase to establish a baseline, and then entering an active measuring state. The entire process allows the system to perform continuous, real-time sensing by translating a chemical concentration directly into a final electrical voltage output. The system is designed to be highly sensitive to small changes in concentration, selective to its target chemical, and to respond very quickly to changes in the sample.

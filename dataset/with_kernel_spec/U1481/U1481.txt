This model describes an AC-to-DC power converter, a common electronic circuit used to transform standard household alternating current (AC) into the direct current (DC) needed by most electronic devices.

The system is designed to take a 120-volt, 60Hz AC input, similar to what comes from a wall outlet. This incoming power is first processed by a full-wave bridge rectifier. This core component, constructed from four individual diodes, effectively flips the negative half of the AC sine wave, converting the back-and-forth current into a pulsating, one-directional flow of DC power.

Following rectification, the bumpy, pulsating DC is sent to a large smoothing capacitor. This capacitor acts like a small, fast-recharging battery, charging up on the voltage peaks and discharging to fill in the valleys. This action smooths out the pulsations, significantly reducing the "ripple" and producing a much more stable and consistent DC voltage.

This final, smoothed DC power is then delivered to a load resistor, which represents the electronic device being powered. The system also includes a voltage monitor that continuously checks the output to ensure it stays within a safe and acceptable range.

Operationally, the converter functions through a series of states. It remains idle until power is available, then initializes and enters a continuous cycle of rectifying, filtering, and supplying power to the load. If the monitor detects a voltage that is too high or too low, the system will enter a fault state for safety.

This model represents a cerebral shunt, a medical device surgically implanted to treat hydrocephalus by managing intracranial pressure. The system's primary function is to drain excess cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) from the ventricles of the brain to another part of the body where it can be safely absorbed, such as the abdominal cavity.

The shunt is composed of several key components that work together in a sequence. A **ventricular catheter**, a thin, flexible tube, is placed into a brain ventricle to collect the excess fluid. This tube connects to a **pressure-regulating valve**, which is the core control element of the system. This one-way valve is designed to automatically open when the fluid pressure in the brain exceeds a specific threshold. Once the pressure returns to a safe, lower level, the valve closes to prevent over-draining.

An optional **CSF reservoir** can be included between the catheter and the valve, providing a small chamber where clinicians can sample fluid or directly measure pressure. From the valve, the excess CSF flows into a **distal catheter**, another long tube that channels the fluid to its final drainage site in the body.

The system operates continuously and passively, without any external power. It functions in different states depending on the patient's condition. In a 'normal' state, the valve remains closed. When intracranial pressure rises, the system transitions to a 'draining' state as the valve opens. The system is also designed to handle potential failure modes, such as a 'blocked' state.

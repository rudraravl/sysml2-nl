This is an automated submersible pumping system designed to manage and remove fluid from a collection sump. The system is engineered to operate autonomously, preventing the sump from overflowing by pumping the collected fluid to a designated discharge point at a higher elevation.

The system consists of several key physical components working together:

*   **Sump:** A collection basin or tank where fluid accumulates. It has a specific capacity and is designed to hold fluid between a minimum and maximum safe level.

*   **Submersible Pump:** This is the core component, an electric pump designed to be fully submerged in the fluid within the sump. When activated, it draws fluid in and pushes it out with enough pressure to move it through the piping. The pump has specific performance ratings for power, flow rate, and the height it can lift the fluid (head).

*   **Level Sensor:** This device is installed in the sump to continuously monitor the fluid's depth. It is configured with high and low setpoints that act as triggers for the system.

*   **Control Panel:** The "brain" of the system. It receives signals from the level sensor and, based on its automatic operating mode, sends start and stop commands to the pump.

*   **Discharge Pipe:** A stainless steel pipe that connects the pump's outlet to the final destination. It's a conduit that carries the fluid away from the sump.

*   **Discharge Point:** The final destination for the pumped fluid, located at a higher elevation than the sump.

The system's operation is a continuous cycle: As fluid fills the sump, the level sensor monitors the rising level. Once the fluid reaches the predefined "high" setpoint, the sensor signals the control panel, which in turn starts the pump. The pump runs, moving fluid out of the sump through the discharge pipe. As the fluid level drops to the "low" setpoint, the sensor again signals the control panel, which then stops the pump. This cycle repeats automatically to keep the sump level within its safe operating range.

The pump itself operates through distinct states, including `off` (idle), `starting` (ramping up), `running` (steady or variable speed operation), `stopping` (shutting down), and an `alarm` state in case of a fault. The entire system is designed to meet specific performance requirements, such as maintaining a minimum flow rate, achieving a required discharge height, and operating with high efficiency.

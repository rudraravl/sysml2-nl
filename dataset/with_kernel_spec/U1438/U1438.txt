This model represents the pioneering epidemiological investigation system developed by Dr. John Snow to identify the source of the 1854 Broad Street cholera outbreak in London. It's not a computer system, but rather a structured, repeatable methodology for conducting a public health investigation. The system's primary function is to pinpoint the origin of a disease outbreak by systematically collecting field data, mapping its spatial distribution, and performing statistical analysis to guide public health interventions.

The system is composed of several distinct functional units that work in sequence:

First, a **Field Investigation Unit** is responsible for on-the-ground data collection. This team interviews households to gather detailed patient records, noting each person's residence, their symptoms, the date they got sick, and, crucially, their primary water source. Simultaneously, they survey the area to document the locations and details of all public water pumps.

The collected data then flows to a **Cartographic Mapping Unit**. This unit creates a detailed epidemiological map—the famous "spot map"—by plotting the geographic location of each cholera case and each water pump. This powerful visual tool allows investigators to see spatial patterns and identify clusters where the disease is most concentrated.

Next, a **Statistical Analysis Unit** uses the case data and the map to perform a quantitative analysis. It calculates key metrics like the total number of cases and fatality rates. Most importantly, it identifies the geographic center of the disease cluster and statistically correlates the case locations with nearby water sources to determine which pump is most likely the origin of the outbreak.

The statistical results are then passed to a **Hypothesis Evaluation Unit**. This unit assesses the evidence to test a specific theory, such as the hypothesis that cholera is a waterborne disease. It identifies the most probable source (e.g., the water pump at the center of the densest case cluster) and compiles the final findings, which include the supporting evidence and a recommendation for action.

Finally, a **Public Health Intervention Unit** receives these conclusive findings. This unit is responsible for reviewing the evidence and implementing the recommended action. In the historical case, this action was the removal of the handle from the Broad Street pump, which effectively stopped the outbreak and validated the entire investigative process.

This model describes a complete Photomultiplier System, a highly sensitive light detector capable of registering even a single photon. It is commonly used in scientific research, medical imaging, and astronomy where extremely low light levels must be measured.

The core of the system is the Photomultiplier Tube (PMT), a specialized vacuum tube. The detection process begins when a photon of light enters the tube through a borosilicate glass window and strikes a photocathode. This component, made of a special "Bialkali" material, uses the photoelectric effect to convert the energy from the incoming photon into a single electron, called a photoelectron.

This single electron is then accelerated by a high-voltage field towards a series of electrodes called dynodes. The system contains a multiplication chain of eight dynodes. When the first electron strikes the first dynode, it dislodges several secondary electrons. This larger group of electrons is then accelerated towards the second dynode, where the process repeats, multiplying the number of electrons again. This creates a cascading avalanche effect down the chain of eight dynodes. By the end of the chain, the initial single electron has been multiplied into a massive burst containing about a million electrons. The entire process is contained within a high-vacuum envelope to ensure the electrons can travel without obstruction.

At the end of the multiplication chain, an anode collects this large burst of electrons, converting it into a small, measurable electrical pulse. The high gain, or amplification, of this process is what allows the system to detect single photons.

To function, the PMT requires several external support components. A stable High Voltage Supply provides the 1200 volts needed to create the electric fields that accelerate the electrons between the dynodes. An internal Voltage Divider Network distributes this high voltage precisely across the dynode chain. The weak electrical pulse from the anode is then sent to a Pre-Amplifier to increase its signal strength. Finally, a Discriminator filters this signal, rejecting low-level electronic noise and ensuring that only pulses representing genuine photon events are passed on for counting or analysis.

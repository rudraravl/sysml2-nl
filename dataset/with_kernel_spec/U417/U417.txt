This model describes a complete medical system for tracheal intubation, a critical procedure used to establish a secure airway for patients who are unable to breathe on their own. It's not a single machine, but rather a collection of devices and a structured clinical process designed for use in settings like operating rooms or emergency care.

The core of the system is the **endotracheal tube**, a flexible tube that is inserted through the patient's mouth and into the trachea (windpipe). This tube features an inflatable cuff at its end, which, when filled with air, creates a seal against the tracheal wall. This seal prevents air leaks and protects the patient's lungs from stomach contents.

To properly place the tube, a **laryngoscope** is used. This is a handheld instrument with a light source and a blade that helps the clinician visualize the patient's vocal cords, ensuring the tube is guided into the trachea and not the esophagus.

Once the tube is in place and secured, a **mechanical ventilator** is connected to it. This machine takes over the work of breathing for the patient. It is configured to deliver a specific volume of an air-oxygen mixture at a set number of breaths per minute. The ventilator also monitors airway pressure and will sound an alarm if the pressure becomes too high, indicating a potential blockage or other issue.

Throughout the procedure and afterward, a **patient monitor** continuously tracks the patient's vital signs. It measures critical parameters like blood oxygen saturation (SpO2), the concentration of carbon dioxide in exhaled breath (EtCO2), and heart rate. This monitoring is essential for confirming the tube is correctly placed and for ensuring the patient remains stable. The system also includes a **suction device** to clear any secretions from the airway.

The entire system operates through a step-by-step procedure that includes preparing and positioning the patient, using the laryngoscope for visualization, inserting and confirming the tube's placement, inflating the cuff to the correct pressure, and finally, initiating mechanical ventilation while continuously monitoring the patient's condition.

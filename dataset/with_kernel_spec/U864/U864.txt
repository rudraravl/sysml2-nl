This model describes a scintillation counter, a complete and highly sensitive system designed to detect and measure ionizing radiation. Its primary function is to convert individual radiation particles or photons into countable electrical signals, allowing a user to determine the presence and intensity of a radiation source.

The system is composed of several key components that work in a sequence. The detection process begins when an incoming particle of ionizing radiation strikes the **Scintillator Crystal**. This specialized material absorbs the particle's energy and re-emits it as a very faint flash of visible light, a process known as scintillation.

This flash of light is then captured by a **Photomultiplier Tube (PMT)**, an extremely sensitive light detector. The PMT first converts the light photons into a small number of electrons. Then, powered by a stable **High Voltage Supply**, it rapidly multiplies these electrons through a series of internal stages called dynodes, creating a much larger and detectable electrical pulse.

This initial pulse, while amplified, is still raw. It is sent through a chain of signal processing electronics. First, a **Pre-amplifier** boosts the signal's strength. Then, a **Pulse Shaper** filters out unwanted electronic noise and refines the signal into a clean, standardized shape for more reliable analysis.

The shaped pulse then goes to a **Discriminator**, which acts as a gatekeeper. It checks the pulse's amplitude against a predefined threshold, ignoring any signals that are too weak (likely background noise) and only passing those that represent a genuine radiation event.

Finally, each valid pulse that passes the discriminator is registered by a **Counter**. This component tallies the total number of pulses over a specific measurement time, calculating the final output data, which includes the total counts and the count rate (e.g., counts per second).

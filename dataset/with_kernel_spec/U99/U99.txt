This model describes a high-bandwidth, implantable brain-computer interface (BCI) system, specifically the Paradromics Neural Interface System. Its purpose is to create a direct, bidirectional communication link between the human brain and external digital devices. The system is designed for long-term use in clinical applications, such as restoring communication or motor function for people with severe paralysis.

The system consists of two primary components: a small, fully implanted device and an external processing system.

The **implanted device** is the core of the system. It contains:
*   A **Microelectrode Array** with a very high density of 1600 electrodes. This array is designed to penetrate a short distance into the cerebral cortex to simultaneously listen to the faint electrical signals from thousands of individual neurons and, when commanded, deliver precise electrical stimulation back to them.
*   A custom **Signal Processing Chip (ASIC)** that is directly connected to the electrodes. This chip's job is to take the weak, noisy analog signals from the brain, amplify them a thousand-fold, filter out unwanted noise, and then convert them into a clean digital data stream at a high sampling rate (30,000 times per second).
*   A high-speed **Wireless Transceiver** which takes the massive stream of digital neural data from the processing chip and transmits it wirelessly out of the body at a rate of 1 gigabit per second. This high data rate is essential for capturing the complexity of the brain's activity in real-time. It can also receive commands from the external system.
*   **Biocompatible Packaging** made from a medical-grade polymer (Parylene-C). This ultra-thin, hermetically sealed casing protects all the sensitive electronics from the body's internal environment and ensures the device is safe for long-term implantation.

The **external system** is a separate unit that communicates wirelessly with the implant. It includes a receiver that captures the neural data being transmitted. This external unit then performs the heavy computational work of interpreting the neural signals to understand the user's intent—for example, decoding the brain activity corresponding to a desire to move a cursor or type a letter. It can also generate feedback commands, which are sent back to the implant to create a sensation of touch or feedback by stimulating specific neurons.

In operation, the implant has several states. It can be in a low-power **standby** mode, then transition to an **active** state to begin recording neural activity. As data is processed, it enters a **transmitting** state to send the information to the external receiver before returning to the active state to continue recording. This creates a continuous, real-time flow of information from the brain to the external processing system.

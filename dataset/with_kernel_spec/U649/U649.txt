This model describes a complete Diffusion Tensor Imaging (DTI) system, a specialized type of MRI machine designed to non-invasively map the white matter fiber tracts—the structural "wiring"—of the brain. Its primary purpose is to visualize the architecture of these neural pathways for clinical diagnostics and neuroscience research.

The physical system consists of several key hardware components. A powerful main magnet generates a strong, stable magnetic field to align water molecules in the patient's brain tissue. A sophisticated system of gradient coils produces rapidly changing, directional magnetic fields that are essential for encoding the movement of these water molecules. An RF (Radio Frequency) transceiver system is used to both transmit radio pulses that excite the molecules and receive the faint signals they emit in return. A patient table positions the subject precisely within the scanner's core.

The system's operation follows a complex, automated sequence. After calibration and positioning the patient, the system begins acquiring data. For each point in the brain, it captures a series of diffusion-weighted images. Each image is created by applying a magnetic gradient pulse in a specific direction, which makes the resulting image sensitive to water molecules diffusing along that particular axis.

Once the raw data is acquired, a multi-stage computational process begins. First, an image reconstruction unit converts the raw signals into a set of diffusion-weighted images. Next, a tensor computation engine analyzes these images to calculate a "diffusion tensor" for every single volume element (voxel). This tensor mathematically describes the three-dimensional directionality of water diffusion at that specific point. From this tensor data, the system can derive key metrics like Fractional Anisotropy (FA), which quantifies how directional the diffusion is—a high value indicates an organized fiber bundle.

Finally, a tractography engine uses the entire field of diffusion tensors as a guide to digitally trace the continuous pathways of the white matter fibers, connecting adjacent voxels based on the principal direction of diffusion. The final output is a 3D fiber tract map, which provides a detailed visualization of the brain's structural connectivity. The entire process flows through distinct operational states, including idle, calibrating, acquiring data, reconstructing images, computing tensors, and performing tractography, before completing the scan.

This model describes a Total Pressure Measurement System, commonly used in aerospace applications like an aircraft's pitot-static system to determine airspeed. Its primary purpose is to measure different air pressures and use them to calculate the vehicle's velocity through the air.

The system is composed of several key physical components. It starts with two specialized sensors: a Pitot probe, which faces directly into the oncoming airflow to measure the total (or stagnation) pressure, and a Static Pressure probe, which measures the ambient atmospheric pressure perpendicular to the flow.

The raw pressure readings from these probes are then converted into electrical signals by a pair of pressure transducers. These signals are fed into a central Data Processor, which acts as the brain of the system. The processor uses the fundamental principles of fluid dynamics (specifically Bernoulli's equation) to calculate the dynamic pressure by subtracting the static pressure from the total pressure. From this dynamic pressure and the known density of the air, it then computes the fluid's velocity, which corresponds to the aircraft's airspeed.

The processed information is then distributed to other systems. A Display Unit receives the data to show the pilot or operator the real-time total, static, and dynamic pressures, as well as the crucial calculated velocity. Simultaneously, a Data Logger records this information at a high frequency for post-flight analysis, maintenance, or testing purposes.

The entire system operates in a controlled cycle. After an initialization and self-test, it enters a standby mode. Once activated, it continuously repeats a loop of acquiring pressure data, processing it to calculate velocity, and transmitting the results to the display and logger. The system is also designed with a fault state to safely handle any detected errors during operation.

This model describes the SPHERES (Synchronized Position Hold Engage Reorient Experimental Satellites) system, a free-flying testbed designed to operate in the microgravity environment of the International Space Station. The system consists of small, self-contained satellites used for developing and testing autonomous technologies for spacecraft rendezvous, docking, and formation flying.

Each SPHERES satellite is a compact, roughly spherical robot equipped with its own power, propulsion, sensing, and computing capabilities. Its primary mission is to autonomously navigate towards a target, such as another satellite, and perform precise proximity maneuvers without human intervention.

The satellite's movement is controlled by a propulsion subsystem that uses compressed CO2 gas as a propellant. This subsystem features twelve individual thruster modules positioned around the body, which release small, controlled pulses of gas. This configuration gives the satellite full six-degrees-of-freedom control, allowing it to move forward/backward, up/down, left/right, and rotate on all three axes with high precision.

For navigation, the satellite relies on a sensor subsystem. An Inertial Measurement Unit (IMU) continuously tracks the satellite's orientation and changes in velocity. To determine its position relative to other objects, it uses a set of five ultrasonic beacons which measure range and bearing.

The "brain" of the satellite is the Guidance, Navigation, and Control (GNC) computer. This computer processes the raw data from the sensors to build an accurate picture of the satellite's current position, velocity, and attitude. It then compares this to the target's location, calculates the necessary forces and torques to close the distance, and determines the precise firing commands for each of the twelve thrusters to achieve the desired movement.

The entire system is powered by an onboard rechargeable battery. A communications subsystem allows the satellite to receive commands and transmit telemetry data, such as its status and position, back to a control station. The system is designed to meet strict performance requirements, including achieving positional accuracy within two centimeters and completing complex maneuvers while using a minimal amount of its CO2 propellant.

This model describes a typical residential home network system designed to provide internet access and local connectivity for a variety of wired and wireless devices.

The heart of the system is a central **Router**, which connects the home to an external **Internet Service Provider (ISP)**. This router acts as the primary traffic controller, managing all data flowing between the internet and the devices within the home. It includes a built-in **firewall** for security, filtering incoming traffic to protect against unauthorized access, and a DHCP server that automatically assigns network addresses to new devices as they connect.

To expand the network's physical reach, the system includes additional components. A **Network Switch** is used in the living room to provide more wired Ethernet ports, allowing multiple stationary devices like a **Desktop PC**, a **Smart TV**, and two **Security Cameras** to have stable, high-speed connections. For wireless coverage, the router has its own built-in WiFi, but the system is extended with a separate **Wireless Access Point (AP)** in a bedroom to ensure a strong signal throughout the house.

A diverse range of devices connect to this network. Wired devices include the desktop computer, smart TV, and security cameras, which stream live video feeds. Wireless devices include **Laptops**, **Smartphones**, and a **Smart Thermostat**. These devices connect to either the main router's WiFi or the bedroom access point to perform functions like browsing the web, synchronizing data with the cloud, or sending sensor readings.

Functionally, the system manages the flow of data packets. The router inspects each packet to determine its destination, forwarding it either to another device inside the home or out to the internet. The switch efficiently directs traffic between the wired devices connected to it, while the access points manage wireless connections by authenticating devices and bridging their traffic to the wired network. The entire system has operational states, including initializing, fully operational, a degraded performance mode, and an offline state, with logic for attempting to recover from failures.

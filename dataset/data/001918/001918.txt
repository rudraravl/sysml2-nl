This model represents the International Space Station (ISS), a complex orbital platform designed for long-term human habitation and scientific research in a microgravity environment. The station is a modular assembly of several critical systems working in concert to sustain life and support its scientific mission.

Its primary power is generated by a large-scale Solar Power System, consisting of eight large solar arrays that convert sunlight into electricity. This power is then distributed to all other onboard systems, including life support, thermal control, and communications.

An Environmental Control and Life Support System (ECLSS) is responsible for creating and maintaining a habitable atmosphere within the station's pressurized modules. It generates breathable oxygen, scrubs carbon dioxide exhaled by the crew, and recycles water to ensure the crew's survival. This controlled atmosphere is supplied to both the Habitation Modules, which provide living quarters, and the multiple Research Laboratories.

A Thermal Control System manages the extreme temperatures of space, using a coolant loop and large external radiators to dissipate the waste heat generated by electronics and human activity, thereby maintaining a stable internal temperature.

For navigation and orbit maintenance, the station relies on a Guidance, Navigation, and Control (GNC) system to maintain its correct orientation (attitude) and precisely track its position. A dedicated Propulsion Module performs periodic reboosts, firing thrusters to counteract atmospheric drag and maintain the station's operational altitude.

A comprehensive Communications System acts as the station's link to Earth. It handles all data traffic, transmitting scientific data from the laboratories and system telemetry to ground control, while also receiving commands and updates from mission controllers.

The ISS operates in a continuous cycle of monitoring and management. It constantly checks its own vital signs, such as power levels, atmospheric composition, and orbital altitude. Based on this monitoring, it can autonomously or via ground command take corrective actions, such as adjusting the orientation of its solar panels for maximum sun exposure, activating CO2 scrubbers, or planning an orbital reboost maneuver. Overall, the system is designed to function as a self-sustaining outpost in space for crew and scientific experiments.

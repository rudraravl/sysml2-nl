This describes a laboratory system for photoelasticity analysis, a technique used to visualize and measure the internal stress distribution within a physical object. The system is designed to test transparent material samples, like polycarbonate or epoxy, to understand how they behave under mechanical force.

At its core, the system works by shining a special type of light through a test specimen while a mechanical load is applied to it. The internal stress in the specimen alters the properties of the light, creating a visible interference pattern of light and dark bands called a "fringe pattern." By analyzing this pattern, the system can create a detailed map of the stress concentrations.

The system is composed of several key components working in sequence:

1.  **Optical Path:** It begins with a **monochromatic light source** that emits a single-wavelength light (specifically, sodium yellow light). This light passes through a **polarizer** and a **quarter-wave plate** to create circularly polarized light, which is ideal for this type of analysis. This prepared light beam is then directed through the **photoelastic specimen**.

2.  **Mechanical Loading:** The specimen itself is mounted in a powerful **loading frame**, capable of applying up to 50,000 Newtons of controlled force. As this force is applied, stress develops inside the material.

3.  **Analysis and Imaging:** After passing through the stressed specimen, the light beam is altered. It continues through a second **quarter-wave plate** and an **analyzer** (another polarizer oriented at 90 degrees to the first). This setup converts the stress-induced changes in the light into the visible fringe pattern. A high-resolution **digital camera** (at least 2048x2048 pixels) captures a precise image of these fringes.

4.  **Data Processing and Display:** The captured image is sent to an **image processor**. This computer uses a phase-shifting algorithm to analyze the fringe pattern and calculate the precise stress values across the entire specimen. The final result, often a color-coded stress map, is then shown on a **display unit** for an engineer or scientist to interpret.

In a typical experiment, the system is first calibrated without any load. Then, the loading frame incrementally applies force. At each step, the camera captures the fringe pattern, and the processor calculates the corresponding stress, allowing for a dynamic observation of how stress builds and distributes within the material until a predefined limit is reached.

This model describes a McLeod gauge, a specialized scientific instrument designed for measuring very low pressures, or high vacuums. It operates not by directly sensing pressure, but by amplifying it through a mechanical process.

The gauge is constructed from glass and consists of several key components: a large-volume bulb, a very thin, calibrated capillary tube connected to the top of the bulb, and a reservoir containing high-purity mercury. It has a port to connect it to the vacuum system whose pressure needs to be measured.

Its operation is a multi-step process for taking a single pressure reading. First, the gauge's bulb fills with a sample of gas from the vacuum system. Then, mercury from the reservoir is raised, which traps this known, large volume of low-pressure gas inside the bulb. As the mercury level continues to rise, it forces the entire gas sample out of the large bulb and compresses it into the much smaller volume of the narrow capillary tube.

This compression significantly increases the gas pressure to a point where it can be easily measured by the height of a mercury column, using a calibrated scale. By knowing the initial volume of the gas (the bulb's volume), the final volume (in the capillary), and the final measured pressure, the gauge can accurately calculate what the original, extremely low vacuum pressure was. After the measurement is calculated, the mercury is lowered back into the reservoir, and the system is ready for the next measurement cycle.

The system is designed to be highly accurate, operating within a specific range of low pressures (0.001 to 100 Pascals) and relies on the high purity of the mercury for its precision.

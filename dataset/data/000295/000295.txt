This model represents a comprehensive digital library of geometric shapes, designed to formally define and classify a wide range of two-dimensional and three-dimensional objects. It serves as a foundational toolkit, much like a geometry textbook or a core component of a Computer-Aided Design (CAD) program, for constructing and understanding complex structures.

The library is organized hierarchically, starting with fundamental concepts and building upon them. At the simplest level, it defines one-dimensional curves like a straight **Line**. These lines can be combined to form closed, two-dimensional shapes called **Polygons**, which include familiar objects like the three-sided **Triangle** and the four-sided **Quadrilateral**. The library further specializes these, defining a **Rectangle** as a quadrilateral with right angles and a **Right Triangle** as a triangle with one right angle.

Alongside polygons, the library defines smooth curves, known as **Conic Sections**. This family includes the **Ellipse**, its perfectly symmetrical cousin the **Circle**, the U-shaped **Parabola**, and the two-branched **Hyperbola**. These curves can form the boundaries of flat surfaces, such as a **Circular Disc**.

Building on these 2D foundations, the system defines a variety of 3D objects. These fall into two main categories: polyhedra (objects with flat faces) and shells (objects with curved surfaces).

The polyhedra are constructed by assembling polygon faces. Examples include the **Cuboid** (a box with rectangular faces), the **Pyramid** (which has a polygon base and triangular sides meeting at a point), and the **Triangular Prism** (a wedge shape). Each is precisely defined by the number and type of its faces, edges, and vertices.

The curved 3D shapes include the **Sphere**, **Cone**, and **Cylinder**. These are defined by their key dimensions, such as the radius of a sphere or the height and radius of a cylinder. The system also describes how they can be generated, for instance, defining a **Torus** (a donut shape) as being created by revolving a circle around an axis. The library also includes more complex variations like eccentric cones and cylinders, where the top is offset from the center of the base.

A key principle of this system is how complex shapes are composed of simpler ones and how shapes are classified. For example, a **Cuboid** is explicitly constructed from six rectangular faces. Similarly, a **Circle** is defined as a specific type of **Ellipse** where the major and minor axes are equal. This compositional and classificational structure ensures that every shape is precisely and unambiguously defined based on a common set of geometric rules and properties.

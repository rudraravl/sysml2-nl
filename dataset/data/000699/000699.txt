This model describes a Murphy Drip Intravenous (IV) Infusion System. This is a common medical device designed to deliver fluids, medications, or nutrients directly into a patient's bloodstream at a controlled rate. The entire system operates on a simple gravity-fed principle, where the height of the IV bag relative to the patient creates the pressure needed to infuse the fluid. It relies on manual adjustment and visual monitoring by a healthcare professional, rather than an electronic pump.

The system is composed of several key components that work in sequence:

*   **IV Bag:** A sterile, flexible container holding the fluid to be administered.
*   **Spike Adapter:** A sharp plastic spike that pierces the IV bag's port, creating a connection to the rest of the system.
*   **Drip Chamber:** A transparent, flexible chamber below the bag. It serves two critical functions: it allows a healthcare provider to visually count the number of drops falling per minute to calculate the flow rate, and it maintains a fluid level that prevents air bubbles from entering the tubing.
*   **IV Tubing:** A long, sterile, flexible tube that carries the fluid from the drip chamber towards the patient.
*   **Roller Clamp:** The system's primary control mechanism. It's a small wheel on a track that, when rolled, squeezes the flexible tubing to restrict or completely stop the flow of fluid. By carefully adjusting this clamp, the provider can manually set the desired infusion rate.
*   **IV Catheter:** The final component, which is a thin tube inserted into the patient's vein, allowing the fluid from the tubing to enter the bloodstream.

The system operates through a clear sequence of states. Before use, it is in a "not ready" state. It must first be "primed" by opening the clamp and allowing fluid to fill the drip chamber and run through the entire length of the tubing to purge all air. Once primed, it is in a "ready" state.

During infusion, a provider starts the flow and enters an "infusing" state, constantly monitoring the drops in the chamber and adjusting the roller clamp as needed. The system can be temporarily stopped or is considered complete when the IV bag is empty. The design's core safety principle is to prevent air from entering the patient's bloodstream, which is achieved by ensuring the drip chamber is never allowed to run dry during operation.

This model describes the Michelangelo Hand, an advanced myoelectric prosthetic hand system. It is designed to replace a missing hand, providing the user with the ability to grasp and manipulate objects in a natural, intuitive way.

The system works by reading faint electrical signals generated by muscle contractions in the user's residual limb. It uses two EMG (electromyographic) sensors, placed on the skin over different muscles (like the flexor and extensor), to capture these signals.

These muscle signals are sent to a central Controller Board, which serves as the brain of the hand. The controller interprets the strength of the incoming signals to determine what the user wants to do. For example, a strong muscle signal might be interpreted as a command to form a "power grip" for holding a heavy object, while a more moderate signal could trigger a "precision grip" for picking up something small. The system supports several pre-defined grips, including power, precision, lateral, and tripod grips, as well as simply opening the hand.

Based on the user's intent, the controller sends commands to the five individually actuated fingers: a thumb, index, middle, ring, and pinky finger. Each finger is a self-contained mechanical unit, driven by its own small DC motor that pulls on a tendon to create flexion and extension.

A key feature of the system is its sophisticated sense of touch. Every finger is equipped with its own force sensor. As the hand closes on an object, these sensors measure the amount of grip force being applied and send this information back to the controller. This creates a feedback loop, allowing the hand to automatically adjust its grip to be firm enough to hold an object securely without crushing it. This is crucial for handling delicate items.

The entire system is powered by a rechargeable internal battery, designed to provide enough power for a full day of use. Operationally, the hand cycles through various states, such as calibrating, being active and ready for a command, and then moving through the sequence of grasping, holding, and releasing an object.

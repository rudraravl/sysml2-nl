This model describes a Maddox Wing, a clinical ophthalmic instrument used by eye care professionals to test for and measure phoria, which is a latent or hidden misalignment of the eyes. The system is designed to assess how a patient's eyes are aligned when their ability to fuse two separate images into a single 3D view is disrupted.

The device works by presenting a different image to each of the patient's eyes simultaneously. One component, an illumination source, generates a single point of light. This point of light is viewed directly by one of the patient's eyes. The other eye views the same point of light, but through a specialized lens called a Maddox Rod. This rod is an array of tiny cylindrical lenses that transforms the point of light into a perpendicular line or streak of light.

By showing a dot to one eye and a line to the other, the system prevents the patient's brain from merging the two images. This "dissociation" reveals the eyes' natural resting position. The patient looks into the device from a standard near-viewing distance (typically 33 cm) and reports where they perceive the line in relation to the point of light.

An integrated, calibrated scale is built into the device around the point light source. If the patient's eyes are perfectly aligned, they will see the line passing directly through the center of the light. If there is a misalignment, the line will appear shifted to one side or vertically. The patient reports the position of the line relative to the numbers on the scale, and the examiner uses this feedback to directly quantify the horizontal and vertical deviation in units of prism diopters. This allows the examiner to diagnose specific conditions like esophoria (eyes tending to turn inward), exophoria (outward), or hyper/hypophoria (vertical misalignment). The system includes safety considerations to ensure the light source remains at a comfortable and safe intensity for the patient.

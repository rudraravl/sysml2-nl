This model describes an Ommaya Reservoir, a surgically implanted medical device used to provide direct and repeatable access to the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) in the brain's ventricles. The system is primarily used to deliver medication, such as chemotherapy for brain tumors, directly into the central nervous system or to withdraw CSF samples for diagnostic analysis.

The device consists of three main components:

1.  **Reservoir Dome:** A small, compressible dome made of medical-grade silicone that is implanted just beneath the scalp. It acts as a small, subcutaneous chamber that can hold fluid.
2.  **Self-Sealing Septum:** The top surface of the dome, designed to be repeatedly punctured by a non-coring needle. After the needle is withdrawn, the septum seals itself to prevent CSF from leaking out or contaminants from getting in. The system is designed to withstand a specific number of punctures before its integrity is compromised.
3.  **Ventricular Catheter:** A thin, flexible silicone tube connected to the base of the reservoir. This catheter is surgically placed so that its tip rests inside one of the brain's lateral ventricles, the natural cavities that contain CSF.

In practice, the system allows a clinician to perform procedures without repeated spinal taps. To deliver medication, a needle is pushed through the scalp and into the self-sealing septum. The medication is then injected into the reservoir dome, from where it flows down the catheter and mixes with the CSF in the ventricle for distribution throughout the brain and spinal cord. Similarly, to obtain a sample, fluid is drawn back through the catheter into the reservoir and then aspirated with the needle.

The system's operational integrity is critical. It is monitored for the condition of the catheter, which must remain unblocked (patent) to allow for fluid flow. The entire system is made of biocompatible materials suitable for long-term implantation. Its operational states change depending on its use: it is normally `sealed`, becomes `accessed` when a needle is inserted, and then enters a `delivering` or `sampling` state during a procedure before returning to `sealed`.

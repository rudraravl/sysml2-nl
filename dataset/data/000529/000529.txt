This is an Alger Brush, a handheld ophthalmic surgical instrument used by eye doctors to remove foreign bodies, such as rust rings or other small particles, from the surface of a patient's cornea.

The device is designed as a self-contained, battery-powered handpiece for precision work. Its main components include a housing with an ergonomic grip, a battery cell for power, an activation switch, a low-speed motor, and an interchangeable abrasive tip called an ophthalmic burr.

Functionally, a single battery cell supplies electrical power through an activation switch. When the operator presses the switch, power is delivered to the low-speed motor, causing it to rotate. This rotational motion is transferred through a chuck adapter to the small ophthalmic burr at the tip. The operator then gently applies the rotating burr to the patient's cornea to carefully abrade the superficial tissue layer and remove the embedded debris.

A critical safety feature of the system is its intentionally low-torque motor. It is designed to stall and stop rotating automatically if it encounters the resistance of the tougher underlying corneal layer (Bowman's layer). This prevents the doctor from accidentally drilling too deep and causing a serious eye injury. The device's operational states reflect this process: it is `idle` until activated, then enters a `rotating` state. Upon contact with the eye, it begins `abrading`, and if too much force is applied, it safely enters a `stalled` state.

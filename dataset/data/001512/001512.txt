This is a necrobotic gripper system that cleverly uses a preserved spider carcass as a natural, pre-built mechanical gripper for handling small, delicate objects. The system is designed to integrate this biological component with conventional electromechanical systems for precise control.

At the heart of the system is the **Spider Carcass Gripper**. It leverages the spider's natural physiology, where legs are extended by hydraulic pressure and curl inward when that pressure is released. In this system, the spider's body (prosoma) is used as a pneumatic chamber. By pumping air into it, the legs are forced to extend, opening the gripper. Releasing the air pressure allows the legs to naturally curl inward, gently gripping an object.

This biological gripper is controlled by a **Pneumatic Actuation System**. This subsystem consists of an air pump, a pressure regulator, and a solenoid valve, all working together to deliver precisely controlled air pressure. It receives simple commands like "actuate" (to open the gripper) or "release" (to close it) and translates them into the required pressure changes.

The brain of the operation is a **Microcontroller Unit**. This electronic controller runs a continuous control loop, deciding what commands to send to the pneumatic system. It receives feedback from a **Contact Sensor**, which detects when the gripper's legs have touched an object and measures the force being applied. Based on this sensor data, the microcontroller adjusts the pressure to achieve and maintain a desired grip force without crushing the object.

In a typical operation, the microcontroller commands the pneumatic system to apply pressure, opening the spider's legs. As the system approaches a target, the contact sensor waits for a touch. Once contact is detected, the controller reduces the pressure, allowing the legs to close and secure the object. The system then holds the object with a controlled force before being instructed to re-apply pressure to release it. The entire system is designed to handle objects with a mass slightly greater than the spider's own body mass and can operate for a significant number of grip-and-release cycles.

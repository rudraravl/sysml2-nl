This model describes a standard mechanical screw system designed for fastening one or more workpieces together. The system's core function is to convert rotational force (torque) into a linear, axial force (clamping force) to create a secure joint.

The screw itself is composed of three distinct parts: a head, a threaded shaft, and a tip. The head, such as a "Phillips" type, is where a tool applies torque to turn the screw. This rotational energy is transferred to the threaded shaft, which is the main body of the screw. The shaft's helical threads are engineered to engage with a workpiece material, transforming the screw's rotation into forward, linear motion. This movement culminates at the screw's tip, which applies the resulting clamping force directly onto the workpiece, holding it firmly in place.

The system's operation is defined by a series of states. It begins in an `idle` state. When torque is first applied, it transitions to `engaging` as the threads bite into the material. As the screw is driven further, it enters the `tightening` phase, where the clamping force is actively generated. Once the desired force is achieved, the screw is considered `seated`, completing the fastening process. The system also interacts with the workpiece, which is described as a material (like steel) with specific properties like thickness and hardness that influence the fastening action.

Ultimately, the system is designed to meet a key requirement: to reliably and efficiently generate sufficient clamping force to prevent the fastened workpieces from separating under operational loads.

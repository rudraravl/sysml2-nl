This model describes a Kinetic Energy Recovery System (KERS) for a vehicle, commonly known as a regenerative braking system. It's designed to improve the vehicle's overall energy efficiency by capturing the kinetic energy that is normally lost as heat during braking, converting it into electrical energy, and storing it for later use.

The system is composed of several key components that work in concert. A central **System Controller** acts as the brain, monitoring inputs like the driver's braking commands and the vehicle's speed from a **Velocity Sensor**. Based on these inputs, it decides how the system should operate.

Mechanically, the system is integrated with the vehicle's **Drivetrain**. The core of the energy conversion process is a **Motor-Generator**, a single device that can operate in two modes. When the vehicle brakes, the controller switches it into generator mode. The spinning drivetrain turns the generator, converting mechanical motion into electrical power. This electricity is managed by a **Power Electronics** unit, which controls the flow and ensures it's compatible for storage. The energy is then stored in a high-capacity **Energy Storage Unit**, like a battery or supercapacitor pack, which is designed to handle rapid charging and discharging up to 50 kW.

The stored energy can be deployed to assist the vehicle's main engine, typically during acceleration. In this phase, the controller commands the system to release energy. The stored electrical power flows back through the power electronics to the Motor-Generator, which now functions as a motor, providing a mechanical power boost directly to the drivetrain.

The system operates in three distinct states:
*   **Charging:** Actively recovering energy during braking.
*   **Discharging:** Releasing stored energy to provide a power boost.
*   **Idle:** Inactive when the vehicle is cruising, stopped, or not in a situation where energy can be recovered or needs to be deployed.

This model describes a Lysimeter, a sophisticated environmental monitoring instrument used for hydrological and agricultural research. Its primary purpose is to precisely measure and analyze every component of the water cycle within a contained block of soil.

The core of the system is a large soil container, approximately two meters deep with a one-square-meter surface area, filled with a specific soil type like "Sandy Loam" and covered with vegetation such as "Turfgrass." This entire soil column sits on a highly sensitive and high-capacity weighing platform, which can detect mass changes as small as 10 grams. This continuous weighing is critical for tracking changes in the amount of water stored in the soil.

To function, the Lysimeter is equipped with several integrated subsystems. A network of ten soil moisture sensors is embedded at 20-centimeter intervals down through the soil column, providing a detailed profile of water content at different depths. At the base of the soil container, a drainage collection system gathers any water that percolates through, measuring the total volume and allowing for water samples to be collected for chemical analysis. The system also includes its own irrigation unit to apply controlled amounts of water to the soil surface, as well as a rain gauge to measure natural precipitation.

All these components work in a coordinated fashion, managed by a central data acquisition unit. This unit samples data from all sensors every 15 minutes. It records the total weight from the weighing platform, moisture readings from the sensor network, the amount of natural rainfall, and the volume of collected drainage water. It then processes this information to calculate a complete water balance. By accounting for all water inputs (rain and irrigation) and outputs (drainage) and the change in storage (from the weight change), the system can accurately determine the amount of water lost to the atmosphere through evaporation from the soil and transpiration from the plants—a key metric known as evapotranspiration.

The system operates in different states, including a continuous monitoring mode for normal data collection, a maintenance mode for calibration and cleaning, and an error state if a fault is detected. The ultimate output is a detailed report of the water balance, providing invaluable data for scientists studying water resource management, crop irrigation efficiency, and the movement of nutrients and contaminants through soil.

This is a Rheotrauma Test System, a sophisticated laboratory instrument designed to study and quantify the mechanical damage to blood cells (a phenomenon known as rheotrauma) by exposing them to precisely controlled physical forces.

At its core, the system simulates conditions that might occur in medical devices like heart-lung machines or artificial hearts, where blood flow can damage cells. The central component is a specialized microfluidic **Shear Chamber**, where a blood sample is subjected to controlled shear stress.

The system operates using a closed-loop fluid circuit. A **Fluid Reservoir** holds a base fluid, which is circulated by a **Precision Pump**. This pump pushes the fluid through the Shear Chamber at a carefully managed flow rate, creating the desired shear stress on the blood sample inside. After passing through the chamber, the fluid returns to the reservoir to be recirculated.

A separate path is used for the **Blood Sample**. The sample is introduced into the Shear Chamber, where it is exposed to the forces from the circulating fluid for a specific duration. After exposure, the sample is passed to a **Damage Analyzer**, which measures key indicators like cell viability and calculates a damage index to quantify the extent of the trauma.

The entire process is automated and managed by a central **System Controller**. This controller acts as the brain, receiving continuous feedback from a suite of sensors. A **Shear Stress Sensor** and a **Flow Rate Sensor** monitor the conditions within the fluid circuit, while a **Temperature Controller** ensures the entire experiment is conducted at a stable, physiologically relevant temperature (typically human body temperature, 37°C). Based on this data, the controller adjusts the speed of the Precision Pump to maintain the target shear stress and regulates the temperature.

Operationally, the system moves through several states. It begins with initialization and calibration to ensure accurate measurements. Once an experiment is started, it enters a running state, continuously adjusting conditions and collecting data. After the experiment, it processes the final data and returns to a standby mode. For safety, it is designed to perform an emergency shutdown if critical faults are detected.

This is a Faraday cup system, a scientific instrument designed to precisely measure the intensity (electric current) of a charged particle beam, such as one found in a particle accelerator or used for materials analysis.

At its core, the instrument features a conductive `CollectorCup`, made of a material like copper, which is placed in the path of the beam to physically intercept and absorb the charged particles. As the cup collects these particles, a very small electrical current is generated. To ensure an accurate reading, a `BiasSupply` applies a negative voltage to the cup, which prevents secondary electrons from escaping the surface upon particle impact—an effect that would otherwise skew the measurement.

Because the current produced by the beam is extremely faint, a high-gain `CurrentAmplifier` is used to boost the signal to a level that can be easily read and recorded. The entire assembly is housed within a `VacuumEnclosure` to maintain a high-vacuum environment, which is critical for preventing the beam particles from colliding with air molecules and scattering before they reach the collector. Furthermore, the system is protected by an `ElectromagneticShield` to isolate the sensitive measurement electronics from external electrical noise.

The system's operation follows a defined sequence. It typically starts in an `idle` state, waiting for a particle beam to be detected. Once a beam is present, it transitions to a `measuring` state, where it continuously collects particles and measures the resulting current. After the signal stabilizes, it may integrate the current over a specific period to determine the total charge before `reporting` the final results. Once the measurement is complete or the beam is lost, the system returns to its idle state.

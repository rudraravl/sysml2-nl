This model describes a Visual Odometry System, which is a type of navigation system designed to determine the position and orientation of a moving platform, such as a robot or drone, by analyzing images from a camera. Essentially, it acts as a "visual GPS," calculating movement by watching how the surrounding environment changes frame by frame, rather than relying on external signals.

The core of the system is a stereo camera, which uses two lenses like a pair of human eyes to capture images with depth perception. The system operates through a continuous processing pipeline. First, a feature detector scans the images from the stereo camera to identify and lock onto distinct visual landmarks in the scene, such as corners, edges, or unique textures.

Once these features are identified in both the left and right camera images, a depth estimator calculates their 3D position in space, determining how far away each landmark is. As the platform moves, a feature tracker follows these landmarks from one image frame to the next.

By analyzing the apparent motion of these 3D landmarks, a motion estimator calculates the platform's own incremental movement—how much it has translated (moved forward/backward, up/down, left/right) and rotated between the two frames. Finally, a pose integrator takes these small, successive motion estimates and continuously adds them up over time. This process maintains an ongoing, updated calculation of the platform's overall position and orientation (its "pose") relative to its starting point.

The system is designed for real-time applications, with requirements to provide at least 15 position updates per second. It is also built to be robust, capable of maintaining tracking even if some visual features are lost, and to maintain an accuracy of within 2% of the total distance traveled. Its operational states include initializing, being ready to start, and an active tracking loop where it continuously cycles through capturing images and updating its pose.

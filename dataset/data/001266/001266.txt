This model describes a self-contained sensing unit designed to precisely measure how much an object stretches or deforms. The core of the system is a flexible, resistive stretch sensor, likely made from a conductive polymer, whose electrical resistance increases proportionally as it is elongated.

The unit is composed of four main electronic components that work in sequence to turn a physical stretch into a digital measurement. First, the **Stretch Sensor Element** is physically attached to the object being measured. As it stretches, its resistance changes. This raw resistance signal is fed into a **Signal Conditioning Circuit**, which cleans up and amplifies the signal, converting the resistance change into a more robust analog voltage.

Next, an **Analog-to-Digital Converter (ADC)** samples this voltage at a high frequency and converts it into a digital value. This digital information is then sent to the **Measurement Processor**, the brains of the unit. The processor uses pre-loaded calibration parameters—such as the sensor's original length and baseline resistance—to calculate the final, meaningful values for strain (relative stretch) and displacement (total change in length).

The system operates through several states. It begins with power-up and initialization, followed by a calibration phase. Once ready, it can enter a continuous measurement mode. It is also designed to detect and enter a fault state if the sensor is over-stretched or an electrical problem occurs. The final, validated measurement data, which includes a timestamp, is then sent out from the unit for use by an external system. The entire unit is designed to be low-power, accurate, and provide high-resolution measurements at a rapid rate.

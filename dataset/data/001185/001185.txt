This model describes a Photomultiplier Tube (PMT) system, a highly sensitive detector designed to convert extremely faint light signals, even single photons, into a measurable electrical pulse. The entire device is housed within a borosilicate glass vacuum envelope, which is essential to allow electrons to travel without colliding with air molecules.

The detection process begins at the photocathode, a light-sensitive surface at the front of the tube. When photons of light enter through a special UV-transmitting window and strike the photocathode, it emits a small number of initial electrons through the photoelectric effect.

These primary electrons are then accelerated by a high-voltage electric field toward the core amplification component: a dynode chain. This chain consists of a series of ten electrodes, called dynodes, each held at a progressively higher voltage. When an electron from the photocathode strikes the first dynode, it knocks loose several more "secondary" electrons. This larger group of electrons is then accelerated to the second dynode, where the multiplication process repeats. This cascading effect continues down all ten dynodes, resulting in a massive amplification of the initial signal, capable of turning a single photoelectron into a cascade of over a million electrons.

At the end of the chain is the anode, which collects this large cloud of electrons and generates a distinct electrical output pulse. The entire process is powered by a high-voltage supply, and a voltage divider circuit ensures that each dynode in the chain receives the correct, incrementally higher voltage needed to create the accelerating field.

The system operates in several states: it can be idle while powered on and waiting for light, actively detecting when photons are present, or in a saturated state if the incoming light is too intense, which could damage the device.

This model describes an Energy-Dispersive X-ray Spectroscopy (EDS) system, a scientific instrument designed to determine the elemental composition of a material sample at a microscopic level. It is typically used as an attachment to an electron microscope.

The system's core operation involves an **Electron Gun** that generates and focuses a high-energy beam of electrons. This beam is directed into a **Sample Chamber**, which maintains a high vacuum to prevent interference, where it strikes a specific point on the material being analyzed. The electron bombardment excites the atoms in the sample, causing them to emit characteristic X-rays. Each element emits X-rays at a unique set of energy levels, acting as an elemental fingerprint.

A highly sensitive **Silicon Drift Detector (SDD)** captures these emitted X-rays. The detector measures the energy and quantity of each incoming X-ray photon. This raw signal is then sent to a **Digital Signal Processor**, which converts the continuous stream of detected photons into a digital energy spectrum, plotting X-ray intensity versus energy.

Finally, an **Analysis Workstation**, typically a computer with specialized software, analyzes this spectrum. It identifies the energy peaks to determine which elements are present and quantifies their relative concentrations based on peak intensities, using established methods like ZAF correction.

The entire analysis process follows a defined operational sequence, starting from a standby state, moving through system setup and data acquisition, and concluding with data processing and the final report on the sample's elemental makeup. The system is designed for high spatial resolution, allowing it to analyze features smaller than a micrometer.

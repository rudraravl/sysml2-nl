This model represents the visual processing system of a human with "blindsight," a neurological condition resulting from damage to the brain's primary visual cortex. The system demonstrates the remarkable ability to respond to visual information without any conscious awareness of seeing it.

The system begins with the retinas in the eyes, which capture light from a visual stimulus and convert it into neural signals. These signals travel along multiple pathways in the brain.

One major pathway, the **Ventral Stream**, is responsible for conscious perception—identifying what an object is. In this model, this pathway is severed or severely damaged, which is characteristic of blindsight. As a result, the system produces no conscious visual experience; the individual reports being completely blind and has no subjective awareness of seeing the stimulus.

However, two other pathways remain functional. The **Dorsal Stream**, another cortical pathway, specializes in processing spatial information to guide actions—determining "where" an object is and "how" to interact with it. A second, more primitive subcortical pathway involving the **Superior Colliculus** also processes the stimulus's location for rapid, reflexive responses.

Both of these intact pathways feed accurate spatial information about the stimulus—such as its location and motion—to the **Motor Cortex**. The motor cortex integrates this unconscious visual data to plan and generate an appropriate physical action, such as pointing to, reaching for, or avoiding the object.

In essence, the system models a profound dissociation: while the pathway for conscious sight is broken, the parallel pathways for visually guided action are intact. This allows the individual to accurately interact with objects in their environment despite having no conscious perception of them.

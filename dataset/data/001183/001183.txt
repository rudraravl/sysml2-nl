This model describes a Photoionization Detector (PID) system, a scientific instrument designed to detect and measure the concentration of Volatile Organic Compounds (VOCs) in the air.

The system's core function is executed through a sequence of integrated components. First, a sample pump actively draws an air sample from the environment into an ionization chamber. Inside this chamber, a high-energy ultraviolet (UV) lamp, operating at a specific energy level of 10.6 electron-volts, emits photons. This UV light is energetic enough to strip electrons from VOC molecules in the air sample, turning them into positively charged ions.

These newly created ions are then attracted to a set of collector electrodes, which have a voltage difference applied to them. The flow of these ions to the electrodes generates a very faint electrical current, typically too small to measure directly. This weak signal is then fed into a high-gain current amplifier, which boosts it into a measurable voltage signal.

A signal processor takes this amplified voltage and, using a pre-loaded calibration curve, converts it into a precise concentration reading, measured in parts per million (ppm). This final concentration data is then sent to an external output. The system also includes an integrated alarm system that constantly monitors the concentration level. If the reading exceeds a predefined threshold (e.g., 100 ppm), it triggers an alarm. The entire device is powered by a central power supply that distributes regulated power to all its components.

The detector operates through various states, starting from a powered-off mode. When turned on, it goes through an initialization and warmup sequence to ensure the UV lamp is stable before becoming ready for use. From there, it can enter an active measuring state, and if necessary, an alarm state. It also has a fault state to indicate system errors.

This is a medical fluid warming system designed for use in clinical settings, such as hospitals or surgical centers. Its primary function is to heat intravenous fluids like saline, blood, or dextrose to a safe, physiological body temperature (approximately 37°C) before they are administered to a patient. This process is crucial for preventing hypothermia, which can be a risk during surgeries or when a patient receives large volumes of cold fluids.

The system is built around a core **warming chamber** through which the IV fluid flows. A **heating element** is coupled with this chamber to transfer heat to the fluid. The entire process is managed by a sophisticated **control unit**, which acts as the brain of the device.

To ensure precise temperature control, the system uses **temperature sensors** to measure the fluid's temperature as it leaves the warming chamber and a **flow sensor** to monitor the rate at which the fluid is moving. This data is fed back to the control unit, which continuously adjusts the power supplied to the heating element to maintain the fluid temperature within a very tight tolerance (±0.5°C) of the target.

Safety is a critical aspect of the design. An integrated **alarm system** constantly monitors the fluid temperature. If it ever exceeds the maximum safe limit (39°C) or drops below the minimum (35°C), the alarm will activate, and the heating process will be stopped immediately to protect the patient.

The device is powered by a standard electrical **power supply**. A **user interface** provides medical staff with real-time information, displaying the current fluid temperature, flow rate, and the system's operating mode. The system operates through several distinct states: it can be `off`, in `standby` mode ready for use, actively `warming` the fluid, `maintaining` the target temperature once reached, or in an `alarm` state if a safety issue is detected.

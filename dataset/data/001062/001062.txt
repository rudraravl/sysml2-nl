This is a Functional Magnetic Resonance Imaging (fMRI) system designed to non-invasively measure and map brain activity. Its primary function is to detect changes in blood oxygenation levels (known as the BOLD signal) which correspond to neural activity, allowing researchers and clinicians to see which parts of the brain are active during specific tasks or at rest.

The system is built around a large, powerful superconducting magnet that generates a strong and stable 3.0 Tesla magnetic field. To ensure image clarity, a set of shim coils actively fine-tunes this field to make it extremely uniform. The patient is positioned inside the magnet's bore on a motorized patient table.

During a scan, a complex sequence of operations occurs. A gradient coil system creates smaller, rapidly changing magnetic fields to select specific slices of the brain for imaging. A radiofrequency (RF) transmit coil then sends a precise pulse of energy into the targeted slice, exciting the hydrogen nuclei in the body's water molecules. After this pulse, a highly sensitive, 32-channel RF receive coil, placed around the patient's head, listens for the faint signals emitted as the nuclei relax.

These faint analog signals are then amplified, digitized, and sent to a powerful computational system. An image reconstructor component uses advanced algorithms (like SENSE) to transform this raw data into a series of detailed anatomical images of the brain. The process is repeated rapidly to capture many volumes of the brain over time.

Finally, a functional analysis engine processes this time-series of images. It performs statistical analysis, typically using a General Linear Model, to detect the subtle BOLD signal changes between images. This analysis results in a functional map, which is overlaid on the anatomical image to visually highlight the specific brain regions that were active during the scan. The entire operation, from setting up scan protocols to viewing the final functional maps, is managed by an operator at a control console. The system's operation follows distinct states, moving from standby and calibration (shimming) to active scanning, data processing, and completion.

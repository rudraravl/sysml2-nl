This model describes a physical magnetic compass system used for navigation and orientation. Its core purpose is to continuously determine directional heading by sensing the Earth's magnetic field.

The compass is composed of several key physical components working together. At its heart is a lightweight, freely rotating **magnetized needle**. This needle is mounted on a low-friction **pivot assembly**, allowing it to align itself with the local magnetic field lines. To prevent the needle from swinging excessively and to help it settle quickly, the entire mechanism is often submerged in a **damping medium**, typically a viscous fluid.

A user reads the direction from the **compass rose**, a circular dial marked with cardinal directions (North, East, South, West) and intermediate graduations. A fixed reference mark, known as the **lubber line**, is attached to the housing to indicate the forward direction of the compass, allowing a user to read a specific bearing. All of these internal components are enclosed and protected by a **protective housing**, which is often sealed, waterproof, and features a clear viewing window.

The compass operates through several states. When first activated or after a major disturbance (like being near a large metal object), it enters a "seeking" state where the needle actively swings to find magnetic north. It then moves to a "stabilizing" state, where the damping fluid reduces oscillations. Finally, it reaches a "steady" state, providing a stable and accurate heading. The system is designed to provide a continuous directional reference and can even account for declination—the local difference between magnetic north and true north—to provide a more precise heading.

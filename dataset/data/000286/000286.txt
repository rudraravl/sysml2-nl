This model represents the organizational architecture of a multi-company collaboration, defining the difference between a complex internal system and a true "system-of-systems."

The real-world scenario involves two independent companies, "Enterprise Alpha" and "Enterprise Beta," each operating their own distinct software and operational systems. For example, Enterprise Alpha might be a national shipping carrier, and Enterprise Beta could be a large online retailer.

Both companies operate a "System A," which could be their respective package tracking systems. Enterprise Beta also runs a "System B," perhaps its internal warehouse inventory management system. A third type of system, "System C," acts as an overarching coordination platform, like a customer-facing web portal or a logistics dashboard.

The model highlights two key configurations:

1.  A **standard integrated system**: This is shown when the coordination platform (System C) combines systems that are all owned by the same company. For instance, combining Enterprise Beta's inventory system with its own package tracking system to show a customer the complete journey of a product from warehouse shelf to their door. Since both underlying systems are managed by Enterprise Beta, this is simply a complex system within a single organization.

2.  A true **system-of-systems**: This is formed when the coordination platform integrates systems from *different* companies. For example, if Enterprise Beta's tracking system needs to hand off information to Enterprise Alpha's tracking system for a final-mile delivery. The platform would combine data from these two independently owned and operated systems to provide seamless, end-to-end tracking. This collaboration across organizational boundaries, linking independent systems to create a new capability, is what defines it as a system-of-systems.

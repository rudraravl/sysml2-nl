This model represents the complete system and procedure for performing an epidural catheter placement, a common medical technique for providing pain relief, particularly during childbirth and surgery. The system's primary goal is to safely access the epidural space near the spinal cord and insert a thin tube (a catheter) for continuous delivery of anesthetic agents.

The core of the system is a specialized **Tuohy Needle Assembly**. This is not an ordinary needle; it features a distinct curved bevel tip (also known as a Huber tip) designed specifically to separate tissue layers and push aside the sensitive dura mater surrounding the spinal cord rather than puncturing it. The needle's shaft is marked with depth gradations to allow the clinician to precisely track how far it has been inserted. It also includes a hub for connections and a removable inner wire, called a stylet, which prevents tissue from clogging the needle during the initial insertion.

Working in tandem with the needle is a **Loss-of-Resistance Syringe**. This syringe is typically filled with a small amount of saline or air and attached to the needle's hub. It acts as a tactile feedback device, allowing the clinician to "feel" the different tissue layers as the needle advances.

The procedure unfolds in distinct phases. First, the equipment is prepared by attaching the syringe to the needle. The clinician then begins to advance the needle assembly through the patient's skin, subcutaneous tissue, and into the dense ligaments of the spine. Throughout this process, they apply constant, gentle pressure to the syringe's plunger. As the needle passes through these resistant tissues, the plunger cannot move.

The critical moment occurs when the needle tip exits the final ligament (the ligamentum flavum) and enters the epidural space, which has much lower pressure. This causes a sudden and distinct "loss of resistance," and the syringe plunger gives way easily. This tactile feedback confirms to the clinician that the needle is correctly positioned.

Once the epidural space is located, the syringe is detached, and a long, flexible **Epidural Catheter** is threaded through the hollow Tuohy needle. The catheter is advanced a few centimeters beyond the needle's tip to sit securely within the space. Finally, the Tuohy needle is carefully withdrawn over the catheter, leaving the catheter in place. The external end of the catheter is then secured to the patient's back and can be connected to a pump for controlled, continuous anesthesia delivery.

This model describes the **Comet Interceptor**, a multi-spacecraft mission designed by the European Space Agency (ESA) in collaboration with the Japan Aerospace Exploration Agency (JAXA). The system's primary goal is to perform the first-ever fly-by of a "pristine" comet, specifically a Long Period Comet (LPC) or an interstellar object that is entering the inner solar system for the first time.

The mission is uniquely designed to be an ambush predator. After launching, the complete system will travel to a stable parking orbit at the Sun-Earth L2 Lagrange point and wait, potentially for several years, until a suitable target comet is discovered by ground-based observatories. Once a target is identified, the spacecraft will use its own propulsion system to leave its parking orbit and travel on an interception course.

The Comet Interceptor space system is not a single vehicle but a composite of three distinct spacecraft:

1.  **Main Spacecraft (S/C A):** This is the "mothership" of the mission. It acts as the carrier, transporting the two smaller probes all the way from Earth to the target comet. It is a fully-featured spacecraft equipped with solar arrays for power, both a high-efficiency electric propulsion system for the long interplanetary journey and a chemical propulsion system for attitude control. It also carries its own extensive suite of scientific instruments, including a comet camera, a dust and plasma package, a mass analyzer, and an infrared sensor. This spacecraft is responsible for all primary communications with Earth, using a high-gain antenna, and for collecting data from the two probes after they separate.

2.  **Probe B1 (JAXA):** One of two smaller probes that will be released by the main spacecraft before the comet encounter. It is designed to travel on a different trajectory, allowing it to gather scientific data from a unique vantage point simultaneously with the other two spacecraft.

3.  **Probe B2 (ESA):** The second small probe, designed to be simple, cost-effective, and spin-stabilized. It has a short operational lifetime of about 24 hours. Like Probe B1, it will be released to fly much closer to the comet's nucleus than the main spacecraft.

The core operational concept relies on this multi-point measurement strategy. As the three spacecraft fly through the comet's coma (the cloud of gas and dust) simultaneously on different paths, they can build a 3D profile of the comet's environment. This allows scientists to study the composition and structure of the coma, the interaction of the solar wind with the comet, and the nature of the dust and plasma surrounding the nucleus.

The mission operates through distinct phases: launching on an Ariane 62 rocket, transferring to the L2 point, waiting for a target, traveling to intercept the comet, and finally, the fly-by itself. During the fly-by, the system enters a "Science Mode" where all instruments on all three spacecraft are active, collecting data during the brief but intense encounter before the main spacecraft transmits the collected scientific data back to Earth.

This model describes a complete light detection system centered around a phototube, a specialized vacuum tube designed to convert light into a measurable electrical current. The system's primary function is to detect the presence and intensity of light by utilizing the photoelectric effect.

The core of the system is the **Phototube**, which consists of three main components housed within a sealed glass **Vacuum Envelope**. This envelope maintains an extremely low internal pressure, creating a vacuum that allows electrons to travel freely without colliding with air molecules.

Inside the tube, a **Photocathode** serves as the light-sensitive surface. When a stream of photons from an external **Optical Source** strikes this cathode, it emits a stream of electrons, provided the light's energy is sufficient. The efficiency of this conversion depends on the cathode's material properties, such as its work function and spectral sensitivity.

Opposite the photocathode is an **Anode**, which acts as an electron collector. A high-voltage **Bias Supply** is connected across the cathode and anode, creating a strong electric field. This field accelerates the newly freed electrons across the vacuum gap towards the anode.

When the electrons strike the anode, they create a continuous flow, which is an electrical current. This photocurrent is directly proportional to the intensity of the incident light. The system's output is this current, which is then measured by an external **Current Meter**.

The system operates in two main states: it is in a **standby** state when no light is present, producing no current. As soon as it detects incoming light, it transitions to an **active** state, continuously performing the light-to-current conversion process until the light source is removed, at which point it returns to standby.

This model describes the Electron launch vehicle system, an orbital rocket designed to deliver small satellites into low Earth orbit. The system consists of the multi-stage rocket itself and a ground control station that manages the mission.

The Electron rocket is a multi-stage vehicle, standing 18 meters tall. Its primary components include a powerful first stage, a second stage for orbital ascent, an optional kick stage for precise orbit circularization, a protective payload fairing, and a payload bay capable of carrying satellites up to 300 kg. The rocket's propulsion is provided by electric pump-fed Rutherford engines, with a configuration of nine engines on the first stage and a single, vacuum-optimized engine on the second.

The "brain" of the rocket is its avionics system. This onboard computer is responsible for executing the entire autonomous flight sequence. It manages guidance by issuing commands for pitch, yaw, and roll, controls engine throttle, and continuously monitors the vehicle's health and status. It gathers data on altitude, velocity, and propellant levels, packaging this information into telemetry data that is transmitted back to the ground.

The system's operation follows a precise launch sequence. It begins with pre-launch checks initiated by the ground control station. Once authorized, the rocket lifts off and begins its first stage ascent. After reaching a specific altitude and velocity, the first stage separates and the second stage ignites to continue the journey to space. At an appropriate altitude where air is no longer a factor, the payload fairing is jettisoned. The rocket may then coast to the correct position before the kick stage performs a final burn to place the satellite into its exact target orbit. The mission culminates in the successful deployment of the payload, after which the mission is considered complete.

Throughout the flight, the ground control station maintains communication, receiving telemetry and having the ability to send critical commands, such as an abort signal, if the avionics reports a critical system failure. The entire system is designed to meet strict requirements for payload capacity, orbital accuracy, reliability, and flight safety.

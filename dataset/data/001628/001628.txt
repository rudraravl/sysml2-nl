This model describes a complete electrical power transmission system, centered around the characteristics and behavior of a single electrical wire. The system consists of a power source, a device that consumes power (an electrical load), and the transmission wire that connects the two.

The wire itself is composed of two main parts: a central metallic conductor that carries the electrical current and a protective outer insulation layer. The conductor's properties, such as its length, cross-sectional area, and material resistivity, determine its total electrical resistance. The insulation layer is critical for safety, defined by its thickness and the maximum temperature it can withstand before degrading or failing.

The primary function of this system is to transmit electrical power from the source to the load. As electricity flows, the system is governed by fundamental physical principles. There is an inherent voltage drop along the wire, and some power is lost as heat due to the wire's resistance. The system is designed to operate within strict safety and efficiency limits, ensuring the voltage drop does not exceed 3% and the wire's temperature remains below the insulation's maximum rating.

Operationally, the wire can exist in several states. It starts in an `idle` state with no current. When power is applied, it enters a `conducting` state, where it actively transmits energy while continuously monitoring its own temperature and current flow. If the current exceeds its maximum safe rating, the system identifies an `overload` state, signaling a potential hazard. After power is removed, the wire enters a `cooling` phase until it returns to a safe, idle temperature.
